My proposed research lies at the intersection of multilingual natural language processing, mechanistic interpretability, and cognitive neuroscience. Written text carries information through two closely related systems: language determines the linguistic system used to express words and meanings, while script determines the visual symbols through which that language is presented. Because the two properties often vary together, their effects are difficult to distinguish. The project aims to explain how language and script information is organised in multilingual representations, and to identify principles shared by computational models and human neural responses while distinguishing patterns specific to either system.

The project asks three related questions. First, does language or script have the stronger influence on a representation? Second, are language and script encoded through distinct, overlapping, or interacting patterns? Third, how does their relationship change across model layers and stages of human reading? To answer these questions, I will develop a general analytical framework to quantify the relative prominence of language and script and characterise the geometry of their representational subspaces; construct a shared multilingual dataset for matched computational and human comparisons; and train and release a suite of multilingual sparse autoencoders (SAEs) for feature-level analysis across languages, scripts, models, and processing layers.

The research employs a controlled comparative design based on two complementary contrasts: the same language written in different scripts, and different languages written in the same script. The base dataset will contain a Training Corpus for representation-model training and a Representation Set for representation analyses. The Training Corpus will be sampled from a multi-source, multi-domain pool covering web, news, scientific, academic, social, institutional, legal, and interactive text. For each language-script condition, up to 50 million tokens will be sampled and divided into training, validation, and test sets. Where natural data do not provide complete condition coverage, controlled translation and orthographic conversion will be used, following established standards and resources. Generated data will be subject to native-speaker quality control.

The Representation Set will contain 100 content-matched passages sampled from Belebele and rendered in the required languages and scripts. Each item contains a short passage and a comprehension question, making the same materials suitable for computational analysis and natural reading experiments. The primary conditions cover five groups in which languages can be written in multiple scripts: Sinitic, Malayic, Indo-Aryan, Slavic, and Japonic. Additional conditions will extend coverage of Arabic, Devanagari, Latin, and Cyrillic scripts, producing 45 language-script conditions in total.

For computational representations, the texts will be processed by multilingual language models and their layer-wise hidden activations recorded. Qwen3 and Gemma-2, together with their available open-source SAEs, will provide reference representation spaces. I will also train multilingual SAE suites for Deepseek-V4.1-Flash, EuroLLM-9B, and Sailor2 using the Training Corpus. SAEs transform dense activations into high-dimensional sparse representations in which only a small number of features are active at a time, enabling analysis beyond the original dense spaces. Separate JumpReLU SAEs will be trained on residual-stream activations from each layer while the backbone models remain frozen. Reconstruction fidelity and sparsification will be evaluated on the test split.

For human neural representations, high-density EEG and simultaneous eye tracking will be recorded during natural silent reading of the Representation Set. Eye tracking identifies when each word is encountered, allowing EEG responses to be aligned to word-fixation onset. Participants will read each content item once and answer its comprehension question; comprehension accuracy will provide a behavioural measure of attention and text understanding. Approximately 10 native speakers will be recruited for each language-script condition selected for neural comparison, with the number of conditions determined by available funding.

A common analytical framework will be applied separately to the computational and neural representation spaces. Quantitatively, I will compare representational differences across scripts within the same language with differences across languages within the same script. Their difference defines a language-script dominance contrast, indicating the relative prominence of the two properties. Geometrically, language and script contrast matrices will be constructed from mean representations, decomposed using singular value decomposition, and compared using the principal angles between the resulting language and script subspaces. These measures will be traced across model layers and successive EEG time windows. Model depth and EEG time can then be normalised to a common relative processing stage, allowing comparison of dominance and subspace-geometry trajectories across artificial and biological systems.

The project is feasible because it builds on established public corpora, multilingual model suites, open-source SAEs, standard orthographic resources, and established EEG and eye-tracking methods. SAE training is planned using 32 Ascend 910B NPUs. The project will be conducted in collaboration with Assistant Professor Ercong Nie, who leads the Language Science and AI Lab at Shanghai Jiao Tong University. His work closely aligns with the project, and the collaboration will support methodological discussion and the computational resources required for model training and inference.

The project is original in bringing the relative prominence and geometric relationship of language and script into one analytical framework that can be applied across representation spaces and processing stages. The framework, controlled dataset, and multilingual SAE suite will form an integrated research infrastructure for identifying representational principles shared across systems and patterns specific to particular stages, model architectures, or training distributions. The results may clarify when models confuse script-specific patterns with properties of a language, providing evidence for improving training data, model design, and evaluation metrics, especially for under-represented writing systems. Comparison with human neural responses may also advance understanding of the emergence of language intelligence and provide a foundation for more efficient and brain-like computational architectures. The dataset and SAE model suites will be released to support further multilingual representation research.
